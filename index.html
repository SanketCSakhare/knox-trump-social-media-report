Trump wins attention with emotion, enemies and restraint. His followers ignore his party chores.

We scored 72,305 of Donald Trump's own posts from 2009 to April 2026 against his typical post at the time. The same habits lift engagement on Twitter and on Truth Social. But engagement on his own platform held steady in 2026 while approval among his own 2024 voters fell sharply: his feed measures loyalty, not persuasion.

Fights, faith and gratitude lift his posts. Endorsements, links and pundit quotes sink them.

Each row shows how much more (or less) a post gets than his own baseline when it touches a theme, holding the other themes and style features constant. Bars are Truth Social (2022 to 2026), the audience closest to his base: dark above his norm, pink below. Open circles are Twitter during his political years (June 2015 to January 2021), when his audience was much wider.

The clearest loser is party work. Endorsements of other candidates, which make up 7% of his Truth Social posts, get 40% fewer likes than his norm, and the penalty grew to 62% in his second term. Posts that are just a link, or that quote a TV pundit, also underperform. His followers come for him, not for his reading list.

The winners are emotional and personal: claims about the 2020 election, gratitude and condolence moments, faith, crime, the military, and attacks on named opponents. Faint bars are not statistically clear.

Style matters as much as topic

Regression on log lift, 25,790 Truth Social and 24,436 Twitter posts, robust standard errors. A post can carry several themes. Theme tags come from transparent keyword rules (see method note).

Some content plays only to the base. Election claims rise on Truth Social and fall on Twitter.

Comparing the two platforms separates content that excites his core from content that travels. Claims that the 2020 election was rigged beat his baseline by 12% on Truth Social but did 10% worse on Twitter. Complaints about his own court cases show a weaker version of the same split (+2% against −8%). Courts and election officials rejected the 2020 fraud claims; the data shows these posts still energise the base but did not travel to a broad audience.

Faith, crime and attacks on opponents worked on both. Those are the themes with reach beyond his core.

The second term sharpened the base pattern. On Truth Social since January 2025, attacks on opponents gained 20% (up from 2%), election claims 22% (up from 9%), and foreign policy and peace-deal posts 15%. Videos and images posted with no text fell to 30% below his norm.

He gets more per post on quiet days. Volume dilutes attention.

On Truth Social days when he posted 1 to 5 times, the typical post got 1.49 times his usual likes. On days with 41 or more posts it got 0.74 times. Twitter shows the same slope (1.20× to 0.75×). Part of this is the news cycle: busy days are often reactive. But the pattern holds on both platforms across about 3,500 days, and it fits how feeds ration attention to any one account.

His best-performing posts were rarely clever. They were moments: the 2015 campaign launch, the July 2024 assassination attempt, his COVID diagnosis, his return to social media. Being present and fast when the country is watching beats a high daily count.

Engagement held up. His coalition did not. Likes measure the loyal core.

Trump's 2024 win rested on a wider coalition than his feed reaches. He came within 3 points of Harris among Hispanic voters and won men under 50, both shifts from 2020 (Pew validated voters). Truth Social is a narrow, older, heavily Republican audience, and its daily mobile users fell from about 436,000 in July 2025 to 261,000 a year later (Similarweb estimates reported by the Financial Times).

Meanwhile median likes per Truth Social post in 2026 were as high as in 2024. Over the same period, approval among his own 2024 voters fell from 95% to 67%, and fell furthest among younger and Hispanic supporters. The groups that decided 2024 were drifting away while the feed looked healthy. Any politician reading their own engagement numbers should treat this as the central warning.

His edge was voice, not hostility. Congressional leaders attacked the other side more often.

We ran 2020 posts from 14 senior members of Congress through the same rules as Trump's 2020 tweets. Trump used exclamation marks in 71% of his posts; the next highest was Elise Stefanik at 36%, and most leaders sat between 4% and 20%. His posts were half as long (19 words against 26 to 42). Yet he mentioned the opposing party less often (20%) than Chuck Schumer (62%), Kevin McCarthy (46%) or Jim Jordan (41%).

Research on nearly 2.7 million posts from US politicians and media found that each word about the political out-group raised the odds of a share by 67%. So attacks work for everyone. What Trump added was a personal, emotional, unmistakably first-person voice that staff-written accounts lack.

Since 2025 others have borrowed parts of it. Gavin Newsom's press office copied Trump's all-caps style in August 2025 and gained over 250,000 X followers in three weeks, though critics called it stale by December. Zohran Mamdani took the opposite route: calm, funny short videos about prices and traffic, with roughly half of views coming from people who did not follow him. Bernie Sanders' Facebook posts jumped from about 2,000 to over 26,000 interactions each in the first weeks of Trump's second term.

A playbook for 2026: borrow the voice, keep the reach, judge by votes

Sound like one personExclamation marks and capitals add 17% to 18% on Truth Social, and capitals added 47% on Twitter. Write in the first person, or post the candidate on camera. Staff-voice posts are the norm Trump beat.
Name a clear opponent, sparinglyAttacks lift engagement on both platforms (+7% to +12%) and across all politicians studied. But the gain is modest and is largest with people who already agree. Use contrast, not constant rage.
Post less, show up for momentsFewer posts each get more. Plan for the 3 to 5 moments a year when the whole country is watching, and be first and human then.
Keep party admin off the main feedEndorsements and bare links cost 11% to 40%. Move them to a campaign or party account, or turn them into a story with a face.
Speak to each group where it livesThe base reads the feed. Young men listened to long-form podcasts; low-engagement voters meet politics through creators and algorithms. One feed cannot reach all three.
Measure persuasion, not applauseTrump's likes held while his voters' approval fell 28 points. Track polling among the groups you need, not likes from those you already have.

What this can and cannot tell you

Likes are not votes. Engagement on Truth Social reflects a small, loyal, self-selected audience. Our coalition findings rely on outside polling, not on the post data.

Associations, not causes. A theme can do well because of the news that day. Comparing each post to the surrounding month and holding other features constant reduces this, but does not remove it.

Data gaps. We dropped 6,953 Truth Social reposts (the file's repost flag was always false, so we rebuilt it from the text), 5,292 posts showing zero engagement (likely collection errors), and Twitter retweets, which carry no like counts in this file. Truth Social counts were captured at varying post ages.

Keyword tagging is approximate. Rules were tightened after hand-reading samples (for example, endorsement posts list issues like "Secure the Border" and were inflating issue themes). A post mentioning a theme is not always about it.
